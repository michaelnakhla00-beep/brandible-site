There's a version of this conversation we hear regularly. Someone has a site. It's a few years old. It's not bringing in calls. They want it fixed, but they don't want to pay twice if something can be salvaged.

That's a fair concern. The answer depends on what's actually broken.

## The difference between a redesign and a rebuild

A redesign keeps the foundation and updates what's on top: photos, copy, color, navigation. It fits when the structure is sound and the real problem is that the site looks dated or the message has drifted.

A rebuild starts over. New structure, new code, new everything. It's the right call when the foundation itself is the problem, not the paint.

Confusing the two is expensive. A new look on a broken site doesn't fix the site. Rebuilding a site that only needed copy and a clearer header wastes money.

## Signs a redesign might be enough

You don't automatically need a rebuild because the site looks old.

If visitors can find what they need, the navigation makes sense, and the site works on a phone, you may only need to update the visuals and tighten the message. Sometimes the offer changed, the service area expanded, or the photos no longer represent the business. That's a content problem, not a structural one.

A redesign is also worth considering when the platform still serves the business. Not every WordPress or Squarespace site needs to be replaced. The question is whether the platform is limiting what the site can do, or only what it looks like.

## Signs you need a full rebuild

Start here: can someone who doesn't know you figure out what you do, who you help, and how to reach you, within a few seconds of landing?

If the answer is no, and you've already tried patching it, the problem is likely structural.

Other signals:

**The mobile experience is broken.** Not slightly cramped buttons. Menus that don't open. Images that spill off the screen. Forms that don't submit. A visual refresh is unlikely to fix a phone experience that was never built to work.

**The site is slow in ways a new design layer won't clean up.**

[Core Web Vitals is a set of metrics that measure real-world user experience for loading performance, interactivity, and visual stability of the page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals)

If people leave before the page finishes loading, and that's coming from how the site was built rather than one oversized image, a redesign on top of the same build is unlikely to change it.

**The platform has become a ceiling.** A template that can't add a booking form, a location-specific service page, or a real quote flow isn't a redesign candidate. You're fighting the platform every time you want the site to do more.

**The SEO structure is a mess.**

[When you're setting up or redoing your site, it can be good to organize it in a logical way because it can help search engines and users understand how your pages relate to the rest of your site](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide)

If every service is buried on one scrolling page with no individual URLs, a new coat of paint doesn't create that structure.

**You've outgrown what the site was built to do.** A site built to show a portfolio and a site built to generate service calls are different jobs. When the business has changed, the site needs to match the work, not just look like a different version of the old one.

## What about SEO when you rebuild?

This is the concern that stops owners from rebuilding when they should. They've earned some ranking and they don't want to lose it.

It's a legitimate concern. It's also manageable.

[Expect temporary fluctuation in site ranking during the move](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes)

That's expected. It is not a reason to keep a site that doesn't work.

[As a general rule: for medium-sized websites, it can take a few weeks or more for Google to gradually start showing the new URLs instead of the old ones (and for larger sites, even longer)](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes)

Plan for that window before you assume the rebuild failed.

[301 and other permanent redirects don't cause a loss in PageRank](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes)

Redirect each old URL to the matching new one.

[Keep the redirects for as long as possible, generally at least 1 year](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes)

Don't pull them the week after launch.

[Submitting a sitemap can help make the discovery process quicker, and it's fine to move your site in sections](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes)

Use that when the new pages are live.

[Change only one thing at a time](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes)

If you're rebuilding and also switching platforms, changing your domain, and rewriting the copy, sequence those. Each major change is easier to diagnose if something needs attention later.

The goal is a site that works. Redirects, a sitemap, and a sensible sequence are how you handle a rebuild without throwing away what the old URLs already earned. A site that keeps bleeding leads because it's broken will not improve on its own.

## The honest question to ask before you decide

Pull up your site on your phone. Not as someone who knows every page, but as a first-time visitor.

Can they tell what you do? Can they see why they'd trust you? Is there a clear way to reach you?

If the answer to any of those is no, ask why. If it's a content problem, a redesign might be enough. If it's a structural, technical, or platform problem, a redesign probably isn't.
