There's a version of this conversation we hear regularly. Someone has a site. It's a few years old. It's not bringing in calls. They want it fixed, but they don't want to pay twice if they already have something that can be salvaged.

That's a fair concern. And the answer depends on what's actually broken.

## The difference between a redesign and a rebuild

A redesign means keeping the foundation and updating what's on top: new photos, refreshed copy, a different color palette, tightened navigation. It works when the structure is sound and the real problem is that the site looks dated or the message has drifted.

A rebuild means starting from scratch. New structure, new code, new everything. It's the right call when the foundation itself is the problem, not the paint.

Confusing the two is expensive. Putting a new design on a broken site doesn't fix the site. And rebuilding a site that only needed copy and a new header wastes real money.

## Signs a redesign might be enough

You don't automatically need a rebuild just because the site looks old.

If visitors can find what they need, the navigation makes sense, and the site loads reasonably well on a phone, you might only need to update the visuals and tighten the message. Sometimes the offer changed, the service area expanded, or the photos are from six years ago and no longer represent the business. That's a messaging and content problem, not a structural one.

A redesign is also worth considering when the site was built on a platform that still serves the business. Not every WordPress or Squarespace site needs to be replaced. The question is whether the platform is limiting what the site can do, or just limiting what it looks like.

## Signs you need a full rebuild

Start here: can someone who doesn't know you figure out what you do, who you help, and how to reach you, within a few seconds of landing on the page?

If the answer is no, and you've tried patching it before, the problem is likely structural.

Other signals worth taking seriously:

**The mobile experience is broken.** Not just small text or slightly cramped buttons. Menus that don't open. Images that spill off the screen. Forms that don't submit. If the phone experience is genuinely broken, a redesign usually can't fix it. Mobile issues at that level often mean the original build didn't account for it.

**The site is slow in ways that can't be cleaned up.** [Core Web Vitals is a set of metrics that measure real-world user experience for loading performance, interactivity, and visual stability of the page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals). [Core Web Vitals are used by our ranking systems. We recommend site owners achieve good Core Web Vitals for success with Search and to ensure a great user experience generally](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience). If your pages are pulling in load times that make people leave before they read anything, and that's coming from how the site was built rather than a single oversized image, a redesign layer won't solve it. [Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): Measures loading performance.  [Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): Measures visual stability.  [Interaction To Next Paint (INP): Measures responsiveness. To provide a good user experience, strive to have an INP of less than 200 milliseconds](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals). The problems that keep Core Web Vitals scores in poor territory are usually architectural.

**The platform has become a ceiling.** A site built on a template that can't add a booking form, a location-specific service page, or a real quote flow isn't a redesign candidate. You're fighting the platform every time you want the site to do more.

**The SEO structure is a mess.** [When you're setting up or redoing your site, it can be good to organize it in a logical way because it can help search engines and users understand how your pages relate to the rest of your site](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide). When the site was set up without thought to how pages relate to each other, or every service is buried under one long scrolling page with no individual URLs, a redesign doesn't fix that either. Structure matters for how search engines understand what you offer. [If your site is hiding important components that make up your website (like CSS and JavaScript), Google might not be able to understand your pages, which means they might not show up in search results or rank well for the terms you're targeting](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide). Hiding important parts of how the site is organized can affect whether pages show up where you need them to.

**You've outgrown what the site was built to do.** A site built to show a portfolio for a one-person shop and a site built to generate service calls for a team doing six figures a month are different things. When the business has changed significantly, the site usually needs to reflect that, not just look like a different version of the old one.

## What about SEO when you rebuild?

This is the concern that stops a lot of owners from rebuilding when they should. They've built up some ranking on their current site and they don't want to lose it.

It's a legitimate concern. But it's manageable. [Expect temporary fluctuation in site ranking during the move. With any significant change to a site, you may experience ranking fluctuations while Google recrawls and reindexes your site](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes). Some ranking fluctuation is normal when a site goes through significant changes, and [As a general rule: for medium-sized websites, it can take a few weeks or more for Google to gradually start showing the new URLs instead of the old ones (and for larger sites, even longer)](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes). it can take a few weeks or more for Google to fully process new URLs. That's expected. It's not a reason to keep a site that doesn't work.

The way to protect what you've built: [Don't worry about link credit. 301 and other permanent redirects don't cause a loss in PageRank](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes). permanent 301 redirects don't cause a loss in PageRank, so if old URLs are redirected correctly to the new ones, the link credit transfers. [Keep the redirects for as long as possible, generally at least 1 year. This timeframe allows Google to transfer all signals to the new URLs, including recrawling and reassigning links on other sites that point to your old URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes). Those redirects should stay in place for at least a year. [Submitting a sitemap can help make the discovery process quicker, and it's fine to move your site in sections](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes). Submitting an updated sitemap helps Google discover the new structure faster.

[Change only one thing at a time. Plan your changes to your site one after the other, not everything at the same time. For example, if you want to move your site to a new domain name, change your content management system (CMS), and update your site to use a new layout, do them one at a time: move to a new domain, then change your site's layout](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes). One thing worth knowing: if you're rebuilding and also switching platforms, changing your domain, and rewriting all the copy at the same time, consider sequencing those changes rather than doing everything at once. Each major change is easier to diagnose if something needs attention later.

The goal is a site that actually works. A well-executed rebuild with proper redirects will recover. A site that keeps bleeding leads because it's broken will not improve on its own.

## The honest question to ask before you decide

Pull up your site on your phone. Not as someone who knows every page and every service, but as a potential customer who found you for the first time.

Can they tell what you do? Can they see why they'd trust you over someone else? Is there a clear way to reach you, or do they have to hunt?

If the answer to any of those is no, ask why. If it's a content problem, a redesign might fix it. If it's a structural, technical, or platform problem, it probably won't.
