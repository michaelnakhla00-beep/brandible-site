# How Much Should a Small Business Website Cost?

If you have searched this, you have already seen two numbers that cannot both be right: a few hundred dollars, and something with a lot more zeros.

They are not answering the same question.

An $800 website is usually a template you fill in yourself, or a very small freelance job. An $8,000 website is usually a custom build: your pages, your offer, a path to call or book, and someone responsible for making it work on a phone. Neither number is a trick. They buy different jobs.

The useful question is not “What do websites cost?” It is “What does this site have to do when someone lands on it?”

## The quote is really a job description

A website is not a poster. It is the place a stranger decides whether to call you, book you, request a quote, or leave.

If that stranger only needs a name, a photo, and an email, a simple page can be enough. If they need to understand three services, see real work, tap a phone number, or get a price without playing phone tag, you are not shopping in the template aisle anymore.

That is why one owner spends $800 and another spends $8,000. One bought a listing. The other bought a tool that does a job.

You can underspend. You pay little, then the site cannot take a booking, looks unfinished on a phone, or needs “just one more thing” every month until you have paid for a real site anyway.

You can overspend. You pay for a store when you take jobs over the phone. You pay for animation, extra languages, or a six-month process you did not need. You hire a firm built for a much bigger company than yours.

The waste is not the dollar amount. It is paying for the wrong job.

## What actually moves the number

Start with what happens after someone visits.

A one-page site for a campaign or an event is one kind of project. A multi-page site with services, contact, and room to grow is another. A store, a scheduler, or a quote calculator is another again. Each of those is more work: more pages to design, more logic to build, more ways for the visit to succeed or fail.

Copy and photos move the number too. If you already have clear writing and real pictures of the work, the project is simpler. If someone has to help you say what you do and make the site look like a real business, that is part of the job, not a free extra.

Then there is what happens after launch. Can you change a price or add a service without calling a developer every time? That is a CMS. Hosting and small updates are often separate from the build. Domain email, extra pages, and stock photos show up as add-ons on a lot of quotes. Ask what is in the number and what is not.

None of that is mysterious. A quote that will not name pages, features, and who supplies the content is not a cheap quote. It is an unclear one.

## What a realistic custom range looks like

Brandible publishes this because hiding it helps nobody.

For the custom sites we build (local service businesses, shops, consultants), typical website projects run $2,000 to $10,000+ based on scope and features. Timeline is usually 2 to 4 weeks from start to launch, longer if the site is a store or the content is slow to arrive, faster if it is a simple landing page.

That range is not “websites in America.” It is what a scoped custom build looks like here: custom design, works on a phone, loads quickly, basic SEO structure, analytics, a clear way to get in touch. Optional pieces (CMS, hosting, booking, e-commerce) push you up the range because they are more to design and more to get right.

A quote calculator on a detailing site is a good picture of the upper end of that thinking. The site is not just “pages.” It has to take a real next step. That is more than a template. It is also not a $50,000 brand campaign.

If a quote is well under $2,000 for a custom multi-page site with booking, ask what was left out. If a quote is well over $10,000 for a five-page brochure with no store and no scheduler, ask what you are paying for besides the pages.

## How to tell what you actually need

You do not need a 20-page proposal to get oriented. You need one sentence.

Finish this: “When someone visits, they should be able to ___.”

Call. Book. Get a quote. Buy. See the work and email. If you cannot finish the sentence, you are not ready to compare prices. You will buy features instead of a job.

Then put the quotes next to that sentence.

Does the proposal list the pages? Does it include the action in your sentence (tap-to-call, form, scheduler, store), or only a homepage that “looks modern”? Who writes the copy and who supplies photos? Does it work on a phone as part of the build, or is that implied? What happens when you need to change a service next month? What is extra: hosting, extra pages, stock images, email?

If two quotes are thousands of dollars apart, the gap is usually in those answers, not in who has the nicer PDF.

A cheap site that cannot complete your sentence is not a deal. An expensive site that still cannot complete it is not “premium.” It is the same problem at a higher price.

## What the investment is for

A website earns its cost when it makes it easier to choose you than to keep scrolling. If people already find you and then cannot tell what you do or how to start, a low price did not save you anything.

If you are still invisible on Google, a finished site will not fix that by itself. Getting found is search and listings. Getting chosen is the page they land on. Do not put both on one line item and then wonder why the number feels high.
