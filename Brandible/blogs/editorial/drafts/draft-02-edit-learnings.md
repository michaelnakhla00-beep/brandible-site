# Draft 02 edit learnings

*Editorial history only. Not website content. Compare `topic-01-ai-draft.md` with `topic-01-human-edit.md`.*

Topic 01 was the commercial-intent test: can Brandible answer website cost without becoming a generic SEO article or an ad. The AI draft already had the right argument. The human pass was mostly about honesty in how numbers and Brandible itself are labeled.

Draft 01 already taught restraint, no fake “most,” no clever lines, no disguised listicles, no promotional CTA pivot, no em dashes. Do not re-learn those here. This file only records what Topic 01 added.

## What changed

**Illustrative quotes vs market data.** AI: “An $800 website is usually a template… An $8,000 website is usually a custom build.” Human: “An $800 quote and an $8,000 quote can both be real. They are buying different jobs. One might be a simple page.” Same contrast. No fake taxonomy of the market.

**Brandible range vs “what websites cost.”** Heading went from “What a realistic custom range looks like” to “What Brandible’s range actually covers.” “That range is not websites in America” became “That is our range, not a national average.” The $2,000–$10,000+ and 2–4 weeks did not change. The ownership of those numbers did.

**Performative transparency.** Cut “Brandible publishes this because hiding it helps nobody.” Replaced with “Here is Brandible’s typical range for the custom sites we build.”

**Examples.** Cut the unnamed detailing quote calculator. Cut the $50,000 brand campaign foil. Neither was sourced, and both leaned promotional. “Quote calculator” in the cost-factors section became “quote form” (a job the visit might do, not a client nod).

**Glossary vs buyer questions.** AI stopped to name CMS and “logic.” Human asked whether you can change a price without a developer, and whether hosting is in the quote. Same information. Less teaching.

**CTA self-qualifies.** AI: if you are trying to see where you belong in the range, Brandible is built to scope that. Human: if you need a custom site in that range, likely a fit; if you need a simple page you can finish yourself, it is not.

**Smaller cuts (not new rules).** “Shopping in the template aisle” became “not buying the same project as a one-page listing.” Copy/photos paragraph lost “make the site look like a real business.” Cost-factors closer stayed. Diagnostic section was barely touched.

## Why it changed

Commercial-intent readers show up with two messy numbers. The article has to explain the gap without pretending Brandible has a survey of what $800 and $8,000 “usually” buy.

Brandible does have real, public pricing. Using it is useful. Writing it as the market rate is not. Labeling it as ours is how a pricing article stays honest instead of becoming a disguised rate card titled “industry guide.”

A client example that cannot be named cleanly, and a $50,000 foil with no source, both exist to make Brandible look like the sensible middle. That is sales structure, even in a useful article.

Defining CMS is agency-blog behavior. The reader asked what they are paying for, not what the acronym means.

A CTA that only invites them in still sounds like a pitch. Telling them when they should not hire Brandible is the commercial version of “stay inside the problem.”

## Permanent Voice Guide rules (from this comparison)

1. Example quotes are hypothetical. Never attach “usually” to an illustrative price.
2. Brandible prices, timelines, and inclusions are first-party. Say “our range,” not “what a custom website costs.”
3. On commercial articles, the CTA must self-qualify, including when Brandible is not the fit.
4. Do not use unnamed client work or invented high-end foils to justify a price.
5. Do not pause a pricing explanation to teach tools. Ask what the quote includes.
6. Announcing that you are being transparent is still promotional.

These are now in the Voice Guide (sections 4, 5, 6, 9, 12, ban list, checklist).

## Article-specific (do not turn into general rules)

- Using $800 and $8,000 as the opening pair. That contrast fits this search. Future pricing articles should pick examples that fit *their* topic, still labeled as quotes, not as a required device.
- The metaphor “the quote is really a job description.” Keep it for this post. Do not require every commercial article to call a purchase a “job.”
- “Template aisle,” “poster,” and other one-off images.
- Merging the add-ons paragraph with the “unclear quote” line. That was tightness, not a structure all pricing posts must copy.
- The exact fit/not-fit CTA sentences. The rule is self-qualify. The wording belongs to this article.
- Dropping “quote calculator” for “quote form” in the cost section. Local to this draft’s overlap with Doorstep Detailer.
