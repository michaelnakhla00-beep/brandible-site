# Brandible Voice Guide

*Phase 0 complete. Extracted from Brandible’s four existing blog posts, plus the homepage, About, service pages, and FAQs. Updated after Draft 01 (Topic 19), Draft 02 (Topic 01), and Draft 03 (Topic 18) AI vs human-edit comparisons. Last updated: August 31, 2026.*

This guide is the source of truth for how Brandible should sound in every future blog draft. It describes how Brandible already writes, not an invented “agency voice.”

The four existing posts show the blog’s rhythm and attitude. The live site (especially homepage, About, and service pages) is currently the stronger version of that same voice: shorter, more specific, more operator-founded. Future drafts should sound like the site talking at article length.

---

## Who Brandible is talking to

A local business owner or decision-maker who already does good work offline and is frustrated that the internet does not show it.

Typical reader:

- Owns or runs a service business, shop, practice, or small team in New Jersey or a similar local market
- Finds customers partly by word of mouth and wants the website, Google, and social to catch up
- Has been burned by vague agencies, DIY templates, or “just post more” advice
- Does not want a marketing lecture. They want to know what to fix and why it matters

Write as if you are sitting across from them on a 30-minute call. Helpful. Direct. No performance.

Brandible should sound like someone who understands marketing explaining what is actually happening, not like a copywriter trying to make every sentence memorable.

---

## The Brandible point of view

Every article should be able to trace back to one idea:

**Make it easy to choose you.**

Supporting beliefs that show up across the site and posts:

- Doing great work is not enough if people cannot find you, trust you, or choose you.
- Clarity beats cleverness. Visitors should know what you do, who you help, and how to get started, fast. Prefer simple, concrete language over dramatic or overly clever phrasing.
- Marketing is a system (website, brand, search, content, follow-up), not a pile of disconnected tactics.
- Consistency beats one viral moment.
- Google and customers reward sites that serve people, not shortcuts.
- Brandible is operator-founded. Recommendations come from running a real local business (The Doorstep Detailer), not from theory. Mention that experience when it explains how Brandible learned the point, not as a stamp of authenticity.

If a draft could have been written by any marketing blog, it is not done. If it could swap titles with another Brandible article and still make sense, it is not done either.

---

## Voice in one line

Professional but conversational. Confident without sounding corporate. Educational without talking down. Slightly opinionated. Practical. Specific. Explains the situation; does not perform it.

**Personality:** clear, grounded, modern, local, slightly blunt, never smug.

## How Brandible actually writes

### 1. Get to the point in the first screen

Brandible openings do not warm up. They state the situation, then the stake.

From the website post:

> A clear, modern site is the strongest asset your brand can have.
>
> Most business owners know they need a website, but very few think about what that website is actually doing for them.

From About:

> This didn’t start in a boardroom.

From the social post:

> There was a time when social media felt optional.
>
> That time is gone.

**Rule:** The first 2–4 sentences should tell the reader what this is about and why it matters to their business. No “in this article we will explore.”

### 2. Short paragraphs. Often one sentence.

Brandible body copy is visually airy. One idea per paragraph. If a paragraph has three ideas, split it.

Do not explain a good point twice. Once the reader understands it, move on. Restating the thesis in every section (and again in the close) is padding, not emphasis.

This is on-brand:

> Going viral is unpredictable. Building a presence is not.
>
> Consistency beats random spikes in attention every time.

This is not:

> In today’s fast-paced world, businesses of all sizes must leverage a multifaceted digital strategy in order to remain competitive and unlock sustainable growth over the long term.

### 3. Talk to “you,” include “we” only when it earns its place

Default address is **you / your business**.

Use **we / at Brandible** when stating a belief, a method, or something the team has actually seen:

> At Brandible, we build sites around one idea: clarity.
>
> Another thing we see often is businesses trying to fit everything on one page. It always holds them back.

Do not use “we” to humblebrag (“as a leading New Jersey agency…”). Do not overuse “At Brandible” — once or twice per article is enough.

### 4. Name the common mistake, then take a position

Brandible articles are not summaries. They argue.

Pattern:

1. What most businesses do
2. Why that fails
3. What actually works
4. What that means for the reader

Examples of Brandible arguments:

- Posting is not the same as building a brand.
- A website is not just a place to list services.
- SEO is not shortcuts. It is clarity, structure, and answering real questions.
- You do not need to go viral.

If the draft only defines a term and lists tips, it fails the voice test.

Be confident about the argument. Do not dress it up as a statistic or a universal. “You might not have a traffic problem” is Brandible. “Most small businesses don’t” is an unsourced census. Same idea; honest scope. “A lot of the time” and “you may already” are allowed. “Most people will see it on mobile” is not, unless you have a named source.

The same honesty applies to numbers. An $800 quote and an $8,000 quote can illustrate that two prices buy different jobs. That does not make $800 “usually a template” or $8,000 “usually a custom build.” Round numbers the reader might see are examples. “Usually” is market data you do not have. Use *might* for illustrations. Use *typically* only for Brandible’s own published ranges.

### 5. Explain in plain English. Define jargon once, then drop it.

The SEO post is the model for technical topics: name the term, give the simple meaning, move to the business consequence.

> SEO stands for search engine optimization. In simple terms, it is the process of helping your website appear when people search for the products or services you offer.

Then talk about customers choosing the competitor who shows up. Do not stay in glossary mode.

If the reader is choosing a quote or a package, stay in buyer questions: what does the visit have to do, what is in the number, can they change a price later. Do not pause to teach CMS, hosting, or other tools by name unless the name is required. The quote checklist is the explanation.

Prefer: *show up, get found, get calls, look trustworthy, take the next step*
Over: *utilize, leverage, optimize your funnel, drive synergies, maximize ROI* (ROI is fine if tied to a real outcome)

Show what customers actually see, think, or do. Do not rely on marketing labels to carry the point. “The menu doesn’t open. They think I’ll try the next one” beats “poor mobile UX.” “No real photos, no names, no reviews” beats stopping at “weak trust signals.” You can name the issue once. Then show the scene.

### 6. Be specific when Brandible has a real example

The live site is specific: Hunterdon County, The Doorstep Detailer, quote calculator, 2–4 weeks, $2,000–$10,000, month-to-month, no long contracts.

The existing blogs are weaker here — they stay general. Future drafts should close that gap **without turning every post into an ad**.

Use a concrete example when it teaches:

- A local service business with no Google presence
- A one-page site trying to do every job
- A Google Business Profile that is incomplete
- Missed calls after 5 pm

You may mention Brandible client work when it is the honest illustration. Do not invent case studies or numbers.

Do not use a client as decoration. An unnamed “quote calculator on a detailing site” teaches less than it advertises. If you cannot name the work, state what it cost, or make it a clear hypothetical (“one quote might be a simple page”), cut it. Do not invent a $50,000 foil to make Brandible’s range look reasonable. The published range, labeled as ours, is enough.

First-party experience is different from client decoration, and it still has to earn its place. The Doorstep Detailer belongs in an article when it explains how Brandible learned the lesson. It does not belong as company history: “before the agency existed,” “a local detailing business,” or other origin-story extra that the name already covers. Use it to advance the argument, not to mention Brandible.

Whenever it fits the topic, give the reader at least one practical test, diagnostic, question, or action they can use immediately. Keep diagnostics strong. Don’t bury them. The test must belong to this article’s argument. A homepage send-around and a phone hire-yourself check are the right tests for a conversion article. They are the wrong tests for an article about whether quality is visible to strangers. Do not default to the same three checks in every post because they are familiar.

### 7. Questions are for the reader, not for filler

Brandible uses questions to put the reader in a real moment:

> Do you look like a business worth trusting?
>
> If someone came across your business today and only saw your social media — would they trust you?

Do not open sections with empty questions like “So what is digital marketing, you ask?”

### 8. Lists are support, not the article

Brandible uses short lists when a sequence or a set of jobs is clearer as bullets (what social should do; what a visitor should understand in seconds). The article still needs an argument around them.

Never ship a “10 Digital Marketing Tips” skeleton with a thin intro and outro.

When several related issues belong in one article (messaging, trust, mobile, speed, navigation, CTAs, journey), do not give each one its own labeled paragraph. That is a disguised listicle. Organize them around what the customer actually does: they land, they look, they try the phone, they hunt for a service, they try to reach out. Merge issues that happen in the same moment.

### 9. Close with a bottom line, then a natural next step

Conclusions should get shorter, not more repetitive. Give the takeaway once, then go to the CTA. Do not restate the thesis three ways and then sell.

Typical Brandible close:

- One practical takeaway (what a working version of this looks like, or what to do next)
- One CTA that continues the same problem, tied to the relevant service

The CTA should read like the next sentence of the advice, not a switch into promotional copy. Connect the specific problem in the article to the service that solves that problem.

When the CTA names Brandible, help the reader self-qualify, including when Brandible is not the fit. On commercial pieces that is often price or scope: “If what you need is a custom site in that $2,000 to $10,000+ range, it is likely a fit. If you need a simple page you can finish yourself, it is not.” On thought leadership it can be the limit of the work: if the underlying quality is not there yet, marketing cannot create it. That is more Brandible than “see how we build sites that convert.”

Announcing that you are being honest is still a pitch. “Brandible publishes this because hiding it helps nobody” is performative. “Here is Brandible’s typical range” is enough.

On-brand: “If traffic is reaching the site and inquiries still aren’t happening, that’s the problem Brandible’s web design and development service is built to solve.”

Off-brand: “That’s the work we do on the web design side, not a prettier brochure. See how we build sites that convert.”

Do not say “buy now” or “limited time.” One primary CTA. No generic “contact us for all your marketing needs.”

The SEO and website posts under-CTA. Future drafts should still include **one** clear next step. They should not suddenly sound like a landing page.

### 10. Contractions are current Brandible

Homepage, About, and service pages use contractions: *we’re, don’t, that’s, you’ll, it’s.*

Older blog posts often spell them out (*do not, it is*). For new drafts, **match the live site**: contractions are the default. They keep the writing conversational without becoming slangy.

### 11. Never use em dashes

This is a permanent hard rule. Do not use em dashes (—) anywhere in a Brandible article: titles, headings, body, or CTAs. Use commas, periods, colons, semicolons, or parentheses. Hyphens in compound words are fine (`tap-to-call`, `month-to-month`).

### 12. Label Brandible’s numbers as Brandible’s

Prices, timelines, and “what’s included” that come from the Brandible site are first-party facts. Write them that way: “our website projects typically run $2,000 to $10,000+.” “That is our range, not a national average.”

Do not title a Brandible rate card “what a realistic custom website costs.” Do not let “typically” drift from our projects onto the whole market.

When you need a contrast the reader will recognize, use example quotes and keep them hypothetical: an $800 quote and an $8,000 quote *might* be different jobs. Do not build a fake industry menu (“$800 is a template, $8,000 is custom, $50,000 is a brand campaign”).

### 13. Adjacent articles must earn their own reason to exist

When two Brandible articles cover related problems, they must not become interchangeable.

Each article owns a distinct argument, set of examples, diagnostic, and takeaway. Related ideas can overlap. The writing should not. Do not reuse the same website symptoms, customer-visit walk, or exercises because they also fit. Define the question this article answers. Keep the writing on that question.

Topic 19 owns traffic reaching a site that fails to convert. Its diagnostic is the homepage and the visit. Topic 18 owns the gap between doing great work and making that quality visible, credible, and easy to buy. Its diagnostic is whether a stranger can see evidence of the quality regulars already know about. Same company. Same reader. Different jobs.

If you can swap the titles and the body still works, one of the articles is not done.

## Sentence-level habits to copy

| Do this | Not this |
| --- | --- |
| “Most businesses treat social media like something they need to get done.” | “Many organizations struggle to fully utilize social platforms.” |
| “People pick up on this instantly.” | “This can have a significant impact on brand perception.” |
| “That time is gone.” | “The paradigm has shifted.” |
| “No templates, no filler.” | “We deliver bespoke, cutting-edge solutions.” |
| “You’ll always know what we’re working on, why we’re doing it, and how it helps your business.” | “We pride ourselves on transparency and best-in-class communication.” |
| “We’re not selling marketing theory. We’re sharing what actually worked.” | “Our proprietary methodology is rooted in industry-leading frameworks.” |
| “You paid for the visit. The site talked them out of taking the next step.” | “Buying more traffic can feel like throwing money at a wall.” |
| “You might not have a traffic problem.” | “Most small businesses don’t have a traffic problem.” (unsourced universal) |
| “They think I’ll try the next one.” | “Which is how most people will see it” (fake mobile statistic) |
| “If traffic is reaching the site and inquiries still aren’t happening, that’s the problem Brandible’s web design service is built to solve.” | “That’s the work we do on the web design side, not a prettier brochure.” |
| “An $800 quote and an $8,000 quote can both be real. They are buying different jobs. One might be a simple page.” | “An $800 website is usually a template. An $8,000 website is usually a custom build.” |
| “Here is Brandible’s typical range for the custom sites we build.” | “Brandible publishes this because hiding it helps nobody.” / “What a realistic custom website costs.” |
| “If what you need is a custom site in that range, it is likely a fit. If you need a simple page you can finish yourself, it is not.” | “See how we build sites that convert.” |
| “Can someone who has never worked with you see any of the quality your regulars already know about?” | Reusing the homepage send-around and phone hire-yourself test on an adjacent article about quality, not conversion |
| “We learned this running The Doorstep Detailer. Doing the work well wasn’t the hard part.” | “Brandible learned this running The Doorstep Detailer, a local detailing business, before the agency existed.” |

Rhythm notes:

- Mix very short sentences with medium ones. Do not write five-line sentences.
- Prefer simple, concrete language. Cut phrases that exist to sound memorable (“the whole game,” “throwing money at a wall”).
- No em dashes. Permanent hard rule. Use commas, periods, colons, semicolons, or parentheses.
- Semicolons are rare; Brandible usually starts a new sentence.
- Almost no exclamation points in body copy. The brand mark is `Brandible!` — that is enough.
- No emojis.
- Bold is for a real emphasis line or a subhead, not every other sentence.
- Headings are claims or jobs (“You Do Not Need to Go Viral”), not labels (“Section 3: Best Practices”).

## Words Brandible uses

**Reach for:** clear, simple, trust, show up, get found, choose you, local, customers, calls, bookings, leads, consistency, structure, presence, next step, real results, what actually works, system.

**Brandible phrases worth repeating (sparingly):**

- Make it easy to choose you
- What you do, who you help, how to get started
- Look as good online as you are in real life
- No buzzwords. No confusing contracts.
- Practical, not hype
- Easy to work with

**Local color is allowed when it is true:** New Jersey, Central NJ, Hunterdon County, “near me” searches. Do not fake a nationwide enterprise tone. Do not write as if every reader is in Flemington either — local examples, general lesson.

## Ban list

If a draft contains these, rewrite before review. This list will grow.

### Instant fail — generic AI introductions

- In today’s (ever-evolving / fast-paced / competitive) digital landscape
- In the world of [topic]
- In this article, we’ll explore / dive into / unpack
- Let’s dive in
- Without further ado
- Have you ever wondered
- Picture this / Imagine this
- It’s no secret that
- Now more than ever
- The bottom line is this: *(as a canned closer; a heading called “The Bottom Line” is fine if the paragraph is specific)*

### Instant fail — empty marketing language

- Unlock / unlock your potential
- Revolutionize / revolutionary
- Game-changer / game-changing
- Cutting-edge
- Next-level
- Supercharge / skyrocket / crush it / 10x
- Leverage (as a verb for “use”)
- Utilize
- Synergy / holistic / robust / seamless / streamline
- Best-in-class / world-class / industry-leading
- Empower / elevate your brand
- Drive results *(unless you immediately say which results)*
- Maximize your ROI *(as decoration)*
- Harness the power of
- Navigate the complexities
- Stay ahead of the curve
- Take it to the next level

### Instant fail — AI tells

- Delve
- Tapestry / realm / landscape / ecosystem *(when used as atmosphere)*
- Moreover / furthermore / additionally stacked at the start of paragraphs
- “It’s important to note that”
- “When it comes to [topic]”
- “Whether you’re a small business or a Fortune 500 company”
- “Not just X, but Y” in every other paragraph *(one contrast per section is Brandible; a full article of them is AI)*
- Em dashes (—). Permanent hard rule: never use them in Brandible articles. Use commas, periods, colons, semicolons, or parentheses instead. Hyphens in compound words are fine.
- Unsupported universals used as facts: “most businesses,” “most people,” “everyone knows,” unless you have a named source. Confident argument is fine; fake census is not.
- Dramatic or overly clever phrasing: “the whole game,” “throwing money at a wall,” and similar pull-quote lines that don’t teach anything extra
- Fake authority: “studies show,” “experts agree,” “according to recent research” with no named source
- Invented statistics, quotes, or client results
- Illustrative prices stated as market facts (“an $800 website is usually…”)
- Brandible’s fees or timelines written as what websites (or agencies) cost in general
- Keyword stuffing the title with `| Brandible` or year spam
- Emoji bullets
- “As an AI” / any model self-reference

### Instant fail — content shapes that are off-brand

- Generic listicles (“10 Digital Marketing Tips,” “7 Ways to Grow Your Business”)
- Disguised listicles: one labeled paragraph per related issue (messaging, then trust, then mobile, then speed) instead of following the customer’s visit
- Glossary posts that never take a position (“What Is Digital Marketing?”)
- Recaps of the four existing posts (SEO 101, why websites matter, why social matters, welcome)
- Recycling another Brandible article’s symptoms, diagnostic, or structure on an adjacent topic
- Hard-sell webinar cadence (“Don’t miss out,” “Act now,” “Spots are limited”)
- Talking down (“Simply put, for beginners…”)
- Arrogant agency voice (“Most business owners get this completely wrong, and that’s why they fail”)
- Corporate announcement voice (“Brandible Marketing Group is pleased to…”)
- The classic agency pricing shrug: “Websites can cost anywhere from $500 to $50,000 depending on your unique needs. Contact us for a quote.”

### Soft fails — rewrite, don’t necessarily scrap

- Hedging every claim (“it depends,” “there is no one-size-fits-all”) without a Brandible recommendation
- Explaining a simple idea twice. Once the reader has it, move on. Do not restate the thesis in the close.
- A CTA that could be pasted onto any article, or a close that switches into promotional copy
- A commercial CTA that only invites them in and never says who should walk away
- Performative transparency (“we publish our prices because other people hide them”)
- Wrapping a real observation in a fake statistic (“most people will see it on mobile”)
- Unnamed client examples used as prestige or to justify a price
- First-party origin extra that only restates company history instead of teaching the point
- Defining tools (CMS, hosting, “logic”) when a buyer question would do
- Headings that are labels instead of points
- Examples that are hypothetical when a real local-business scenario would do
- Over-capitalizing Common Marketing Words
- Marketing terminology doing the work that a scene should do (“poor mobile UX,” “weak trust signals,” “suboptimal CTA”) with no picture of what the visitor sees or does

## Brandible vs generic AI (side by side)

**Generic AI**

> In today’s ever-evolving digital landscape, unlocking your brand’s potential is more important than ever. In this article, we’ll explore key strategies to revolutionize your online presence and take your business to the next level.

**Brandible**

> You might not have a traffic problem. You might have a website problem. People land on the site, can’t tell what to do next, and leave.

**Generic AI**

> SEO is a multifaceted process that encompasses on-page optimization, off-page signals, and technical considerations in order to improve visibility.

**Brandible**

> If your business does not show up when someone searches for what you offer, they choose the competitor who does. Visibility is not random. It is earned.

**Generic AI**

> Ready to transform your digital journey? Contact us today to get started!

**Brandible**

> If traffic is reaching the site and inquiries still aren’t happening, that’s the problem Brandible’s web design and development service is built to solve. [See how we approach it](/services/web-design/)

**Generic AI**

> Websites can cost anywhere from $500 to $50,000 depending on your unique needs. Contact us for a quote!

**Brandible**

> An $800 quote and an $8,000 quote can both be real. They are buying different jobs. Brandible’s custom sites typically run $2,000 to $10,000+. That is our range, not a national average. If you need a simple page you can finish yourself, we are not the fit.

## What Brandible should sound like it knows

Knowledgeable:

- Names the real failure mode (random posting, one-page websites, ads with no tracking, incomplete Google Business Profile)
- Shows what the visitor sees, thinks, or does, not just the marketing label for it
- Ties tactics back to trust, calls, bookings, or leads
- Distinguishes local search from generic “SEO”
- Will say “this is not how it works” when the popular advice is wrong
- Gives the reader something they can check this week, and that check belongs to this article’s argument
- Labels Brandible prices and timelines as ours, not as what the market charges

Not arrogant:

- Does not mock the reader
- Does not pretend every business needs a $50k rebrand, and does not invent a $50k foil to make a real quote look modest
- Does not claim Brandible is the biggest or the only competent shop
- Offers a next step, including “this is not for you”
- Does not need every sentence to be a pull quote

The About page is the tone ceiling:

> We don’t aim to be the biggest agency in NJ — just the one our clients actually trust.

## Brandible Voice Test Checklist

Score every AI draft against this list before human editing. A draft needs **all items passing** to be worth editing. One fail = revise, don’t polish.

Use the standalone copy in `voice-test-checklist.md` while reviewing.

| # | Check | Pass when… | Fail when… |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Intro gets to the point** | First 2–4 sentences state the problem or claim. A busy owner knows why to keep reading. | Warm-up, definition dump, or “in this article we’ll cover.” |
| 2 | **Conversational, not sloppy** | Sounds like a smart person on a call. Contractions are fine. | Slang, jokes that punch down, or stiff corporate prose. |
| 3 | **No generic AI phrasing** | Ban list is clean. No filler frames. No em dashes. Language is concrete, not clever. | Any instant-fail phrase, disguised listicle, or pull-quote metaphor. |
| 4 | **Clear argument** | You can state the thesis in one sentence. Sections advance that thesis. The argument is confident without fake “most people” statistics or illustrative prices treated as market data. | The piece only summarizes “what X is,” claims a census it can’t support, or says what an $800 site “usually” is. |
| 5 | **Practical examples** | At least one concrete situation a local owner recognizes. Hypothetical quotes are labeled as might/can. Brandible work is named or omitted. First-party stories teach the lesson, not the company history. Examples are not copied wholesale from an adjacent Brandible article. | Pure abstraction, unnamed client decoration, invented foils, origin-story padding, marketing terms with no scene, or recycled examples from a sibling post. |
| 6 | **Knowledgeable, not arrogant** | Speaks from experience. Corrects bad advice without insulting the reader. Sounds like someone explaining what is happening. | “Most owners fail because they’re lazy” energy, empty guru claims, or copywriter performance. |
| 7 | **No overexplaining** | Each idea is said once. The close does not restate the thesis. | The same point restated two or three ways, especially at the end. |
| 8 | **Useful to a decision-maker** | Reader could change a page, a listing, a budget question, or a next step after reading. There is at least one practical test, question, or action, and it belongs to this article’s argument. | They only learned that “marketing is important,” or the diagnostic was copied from a related post because it also fit. |
| 9 | **Natural CTA** | One relevant service, earned by the specific problem. Reads like the next sentence of the advice. On commercial pieces, the reader can tell if they are a fit, including when they are not. | Hard sell, promotional pivot, performative honesty, or a CTA that would fit any post. |
| 10 | **Would Brandible publish this?** | You can imagine it on brandiblemg.com next to the site’s current copy. It could not swap titles with an adjacent Brandible article and still work. | It sounds like a stock SEO blog with a Brandible logo on it, or like a rewrite of a post Brandible already has. |

**Overall:** Publish-ready only if 10/10 pass after human edit. Phase 0 drafts are expected to need edits; they are not expected to fail the checklist on voice.

## How to use this guide

1. Read this file before writing or reviewing a draft.
2. Run `voice-test-checklist.md` on the finished draft.
3. Follow `editorial-standard.md` for structure, sourcing, CTAs, and CMS fields.
4. Pick topics from `topic-queue.md` — do not improvise another awareness-stage explainer.

The goal is not “AI that writes blogs.” The goal is writing Brandible would actually put its name on.
